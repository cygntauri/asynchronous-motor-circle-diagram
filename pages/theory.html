Documentation and Formulas

1. Speed and Slip Formulas :

The operation of the Asynchronous Machine (AM) fundamentally requires the rotor speed ($n$) to be lower than the synchronous speed ($n_s$) of the stator's rotating magnetic field. This necessary speed difference is defined by the slip ($g$). If the rotor achieved synchronous speed ($n=n_s$), there would be no relative motion between the rotor conductors and the magnetic field; consequently, no current would be induced in the rotor, and no motor torque would be generated. The slip is the governing factor for the frequency of the induced rotor currents ($f_2$) and electromotive forces (EMFs).

\[g = \dfrac{n_s - n}{n_s}\]

Here, $n$ is the mechanical rotational speed of the rotor in rev/s.

\[f_2 = g\, f_1\]
\[\omega_r = g\, \omega_s\]
\[\Omega_r = \dfrac{\omega_r}{p} = g\, \Omega_s\]

Where $p$ is the number of pole pairs.

2. Electromotive Force (E.M.F.) :

The Asynchronous Machine operates electrically like a transformer, especially at standstill ($g=1$). The induced E.M.F. in the stator ($E_1$) follows the standard transformer primary equation. Crucially, when the rotor is moving, its induced E.M.F. ($E_2$) is reduced by the slip factor ($g$) compared to its value at rest ($E_{20}$). The transformation ratio ($m$) links the stator and rotor E.M.F.s, incorporating the winding factors (KAPP coefficients $K_s, K_r$) and the number of conductors ($N_s, N_r$). In an open rotor test, neglecting primary voltage drops allows this ratio to be approximated by the voltage ratio $V_s/V_r$.

\[E_1 = 4.44\, N_1\, f_1\, \Phi\]

$N_1$ = number of stator turns, $\Phi$ = flux.

\[E_{20} = 4.44\, N_2\, f\, \Phi\]
\[E_2 = g\, E_{20}\]
\[m = \dfrac{E_s}{E_r} = \dfrac{K_s N_s}{K_r N_r}\]

Approximately (neglecting primary voltage drops): $m \cong V_s / V_r$.

3. Equivalent Electrical Circuit Equations :

The governing equations rely on simplifying assumptions like neglecting magnetic saturation, assuming symmetrical construction, and sinusoidal flux distribution. For steady-state analysis, the complex mathematical challenge is uniting the stator and rotor domains (which operate at different frequencies, $\omega_s$ and $g \omega_s$, respectively) into a single equivalent circuit. This is conventionally resolved by referring all rotor quantities to the stator frequency ($\omega_s$), necessitating the modification of the rotor resistance term from $R_r$ to $R_r/g$ in the equivalent impedance calculation. This simplification allows the use of complex notation to analytically calculate the total impedance ($Z_s$) and resulting currents.

\[R_s I_s + j L_s \omega_s I_s + j M \omega_s I_r = V_s\]
\[\dfrac{R_r}{g} I_r + j L_r \omega_s I_r + j M \omega_s I_s = 0\]
  • Magnetizing reactance: $X_{\mu} = m M \omega_s$
  • Magnetizing current: $I_{s\mu} = I_s - I_r'$
  • Referred rotor resistance: $R_r' = m^2 R_r$
  • Referred rotor current: $I_r' = -I_r / m$
  • Total leakage impedance referred to stator: $l_s' = l_s + l_r'$
\[Z_s = \dfrac{(R_{\mu} + j X_{\mu})(R_s + R_r'/g + j l_s' \omega_s)}{R_{\mu} + j X_{\mu} + R_s + R_r'/g + j l_s' \omega_s}\]
\[I_s = \dfrac{V_s}{Z_s}\]
\[I_r' = \dfrac{V_s}{\sqrt{(R_s + R_r'/g)^2 + (l_s' \omega_s)^2}}\]

4. Power and Efficiency Formulas :

Power analysis follows the energy flow from absorbed electrical power ($P_s$) to useful mechanical power ($P_u$). The power transmitted across the air gap ($P_s'$), representing $P_s$ minus all stator losses, is the key internal quantity. A foundational principle dictates that rotor Joule losses ($p_{jr}$) are directly proportional to this transmitted power ($P_s'$) by the slip factor ($g$). Mechanical power ($P_{mec}$) is derived by subtracting $p_{jr}$ from $P_s'$, leading ultimately to the useful power ($P_u$) after subtracting mechanical losses ($p_{mec}$). Efficiency ($\eta$) is simply the final output power ($P_u$) divided by the absorbed input power ($P_s$).

\[P_s = 3\, \Re(Z_s)\, I_s^2\]

Absorbed power minus stator losses gives power transmitted to rotor:

\[P_s' = P_s - p_{fer} - p_{js}\]
\[P_s' = 3\, \dfrac{R_r'}{g}\, I_r'^2 = \dfrac{p_{jr}}{g}\]
\[p_{jr} = g\, P_s'\]
\[p_{jr} = 3\, R_r\, I_r^2 = 3\, R_r'\, I_r'^2\]
\[P_{mec} = P_s' - p_{jr} = (1 - g)\, P_s'\]
\[P_u = P_{mec} - p_{mec} = (1 - g)\, P_s' - p_{mec}\]

Also $P_u = \Gamma_u \Omega$, where $\Gamma_u$ is useful torque and $\Omega$ is rotor angular speed.

\[\eta = \dfrac{P_u}{P_s} = \dfrac{[P_s - p_{fer} - p_{js}](1-g) - p_{mec}}{P_s}\]

5. Torque Formulas :

The mechanical or synchronous torque ($\Gamma_{mec}$) is calculated simply by relating the power transmitted to the rotor ($P_s'$) to the synchronous angular speed ($\Omega_s$). The comprehensive analytical equation for $\Gamma_{mec}$ as a function of slip ($g$) forms the basis of the mechanical characteristic. Key results derived from this relation show that, while the maximum torque ($\Gamma_{max}$) is independent of rotor resistance ($R_r'$), the specific slip ($g_{\Gamma \, max}$) at which this maximum torque is reached is directly proportional to $R_r'$.

\[\Gamma_{mec} = \dfrac{P_{mec}}{\Omega} = \dfrac{P_s'}{\Omega_s}\]
\[\Gamma_{mec} = \dfrac{3\, R_r'\, V_s^2}{\Omega_s\, g}\times\dfrac{1}{\big[(R_s + R_r'/g)^2 + (l_s' \omega_s)^2\big]}\]
\[\Gamma_d = \dfrac{3\, R_r'\, V_s^2}{\Omega_s}\times\dfrac{1}{\big[(R_s + R_r')^2 + (l_s' \omega_s)^2\big]}\]

Starting torque can also be written using rotor Joule losses at g=1: $\Gamma_d = P_s'_{g=1} / \Omega_s = p_{jr} / \Omega_s$.

\[R_r' = \sqrt{R_s^2 + (l_s' \omega_s)^2}\]
\[g_{\Gamma\,max} = \dfrac{R_r'}{\sqrt{R_s^2 + (l_s' \omega_s)^2}}\]
\[\Gamma_{max} = \dfrac{3\, V_s^2}{2\, \Omega_s}\times\dfrac{1}{\big[R_s + \sqrt{R_s^2 + (l_s' \omega_s)^2}\big]}\]

6. Starting and Characteristic Modification Formulas

Starting methods are essential to limit the high current absorbed at startup while ensuring sufficient torque. Techniques involve modifying the rotor circuit resistance (for wound rotor motors, using a secondary rheostat) or reducing the primary supply voltage (for cage motors). Because the starting torque ($\Gamma_d$) is proportional to the square of the supply voltage ($V_s$), voltage reduction significantly impacts torque. However, in stable, low-slip operation, maintaining a constant mechanical torque necessitates that the slip must increase proportionally to the square of the inverse voltage reduction ratio.

Starting by Secondary Rheostat (Wound Rotor Motors)

\[k^n = \dfrac{R_r + R_h}{R_r}\]
\[\dfrac{g_1}{g_2} = \dfrac{R_r'}{R_r' + R_h}\]

Starting by Primary Voltage Reduction (Squirrel Cage Motors)

With series stator resistance $R_h$:

\[I_d = \dfrac{V_s}{\sqrt{R_s^2 + X_s^2}}\]
\[I_d' = \dfrac{V_s}{\sqrt{(R_h + R_s)^2 + X_s^2}}\]
\[\Gamma_d' = \left(\dfrac{V_s'}{V_s}\right)^2 \Gamma_d = \left(\dfrac{I_d'}{I_d}\right)^2 \Gamma_d\]

Star-Delta starting (phase voltage reduced by $\sqrt{3}$):

\[I_d' = \dfrac{I_d}{\sqrt{3}}\]
\[\Gamma_d' = \dfrac{\Gamma_d}{3}\]

Modification by Voltage Variation (Approximation)

\[\dfrac{g_2}{g_1} \cong \left(\dfrac{V_{s1}}{V_{s2}}\right)^2\]

Valid for nominal operation at small slip (constant torque assumption).

7. Power Ratios and Losses :

Graphical circle-diagram projections give the following relations:

  • Iron losses: $p_{fer} = 3 V_s \cdot M_0M_1$
  • Stator Joule losses: $p_{js} = 3 V_s \cdot M_1M_2$
  • Rotor Joule losses: $p_{jr} = 3 V_s \cdot M_2M_3$
  • Mechanical losses: $p_{mec} = 3 V_s \cdot M_3M_4$
  • Useful Power: $P_u = 3 V_s \cdot M_4M$
  • Absorbed Power: $P_s = 3 V_s \cdot M_0M$
  • Power transmitted to rotor: $P_s' = 3 V_s \cdot M_2M$
  • Mechanical torque: $\Gamma_{mec} = \dfrac{3 V_s \cdot M_2M}{\Omega_s}$
  • Slip (graphical): $g = \dfrac{M_2M_3}{M_2M}$
  • Efficiency: $\eta = \dfrac{M_4M}{M_0M}$

Separating Iron and Mechanical Losses (No-Load Test)

\[P_{sv} \cong p_{fer} + p_{mec}\]
\[P_{sv} - P_{svo} \cong p_{mec}\]
